### `feature.composite_value_yield`

**How to read it.** What the company earns for each dollar of its value, on two yields averaged: operating profit over enterprise value and free cash flow over market value. 0.08 is 8 cents a year; above 0.10 is cheap by either measure, below 0.04 is dear. Value is high-is-cheap here, the opposite of ev_ebit, so a screen for cheap names asks for a high number.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cheap on earnings and cash flow | `gte 0.08` | soft | 0.25 x the threshold | - |

**When the reading lies**

- A formalisation, not the paper's composite. The research composites use book to market, earnings, cash flow and sales growth. This has two yields only and averages the yields themselves, so a very high yield in one part dominates the mean.
- A very high yield is often a peak-earnings cyclical or a company the market expects to shrink: check feature.revenue_cagr_3y and feature.cash_op_profitability.
- Null for a company with an operating loss, a negative enterprise value, no capex line, a stale statement or no market value, and for banks. A missing value is a gap, not a pass.
- Enterprise value ignores minority interest and preferred stock, and both yields use the latest filings, up to four months old.

Sources: Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny (1994), Journal of Finance 49(5), Contrarian Investment, Extrapolation, and Risk; Asness and Frazzini (2013), Journal of Portfolio Management 39(4), The Devil in HML's Details

### `rollup.qmj@v1.qmj_score`

**How to read it.** Quality minus junk as a z-score across the day's cross-section: 0 is the middle, 1 is one standard deviation above, -1 below. High is quality (profitable, cash-generating, little debt and distress risk), low is junk. 1 and above is the top sixth of the cross-section; the idea (Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen) is to prefer quality and avoid junk at any price.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a high-quality name | `gte 0.5` | soft | 0.3 | - |

**When the reading lies**

- Built from what is stored. Profitability is complete (gross profit over assets, ROE, ROA, cash flow over assets, gross margin, low accruals). Safety is only leverage and distress. The paper's growth, beta and earnings-volatility parts are not built, so rollup.qmj@v1.safety_z does not know how steady the earnings are.
- Relative to the session: the score falls when others improve; it has no level and cannot be compared across dates. It ranks across all names, so an asset-light software company outranks a utility on margins.
- Needs enough profitability parts (four of six) and a cross-section of at least 100 names; a name without them has no score (a gap, not a pass). Banks are scored on the parts they have.
- Quality is not cheapness: compare feature.pb_ratio and feature.ev_ebit. Statement figures enter from the session after SEC acceptance of the filing and can be four months old.

Sources: Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen (2019), Review of Accounting Studies 24(1), Quality Minus Junk; Novy-Marx (2014), Journal of Financial Economics 112(1), The Quality Dimension of Value Investing

### `feature.net_issuance_pct_12m`

**How to read it.** The log change of the share count over a year, the form Pontiff and Woodgate use. A value of -0.03 is about 3% of the shares bought back and +0.10 about 10% more shares issued. Negative is a net buyer of its own stock and around 0 is stable. Above +0.05 is a steady issuer. Above +0.20 is a heavy one: an offering, an acquisition in stock or heavy compensation. In the research the issuers underperform and the repurchasers outperform. It ranks like feature.shares_change_yoy. It is the form the issuance edge sorts on.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| buying back shares | `lte -0.01` | soft | 0.01 | - |
| no heavy issuance | `lte 0.1` | soft | 0.03 | - |

**When the reading lies**

- The counts are the company total from the filings. A cover-page count is compared with a cover-page count and a weighted average with a weighted average, so a company that began tagging the cover page recently is null.
- An acquisition paid in shares, a spin-off or a reverse merger changes the count without being a financing decision; a split is adjusted away.
- A shrinking count can hide issuance: companies buy back what compensation issues, so a flat count is not a company that does nothing. A buyback funded by debt is not strength (see feature.debt_to_equity).
- Null for funds, companies without two counts a year apart (recent IPOs and filers) and a stale count.

Sources: Pontiff and Woodgate (2008), Journal of Finance 63(2), Share Issuance and Cross-Sectional Returns

### `rollup.qmj@v1.profitability_z`

**How to read it.** How profitable a company is against the session's other companies, in standard deviations. It averages the z-scores of six measures: gross profit to assets, ROE, ROA, operating cash flow to assets, gross margin and low accruals. 0 is the middle, 1 is one standard deviation above, -1 below. It is the profitability half of the quality-minus-junk score.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a more profitable company than most | `gte 0.5` | soft | 0.25 | illustrative screen, not a published cut-off: half a standard deviation above the session middle; the paper ranks, it sets no level. Read with rollup.qmj@v1.safety_z |

**When the reading lies**

- A cross-section statistic: it moves when other companies move and carries no level. It is null when fewer than 100 companies have a value that session.
- Null for ETFs, funds, companies with no CIK or a non-USD report, and when a statement is more than 480 days old. A hard 'gte' drops these names.
- Needs at least 4 of the 6 measures; a company with an unreported line is scored on fewer, and a bank or insurer is often unscored. Read rollup.qmj@v1.balance_sheet_age_days for how old the statements are.

Sources: Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen (2019), Quality Minus Junk, Review of Accounting Studies 24: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-018-9470-2

### `rollup.qmj@v1.safety_z`

**How to read it.** How safe a company's balance sheet is against the session's others, in standard deviations: the average of the z-scores of low debt to equity and low ohlson_o (a distress score). 1 is one standard deviation safer than the middle; -1 riskier. It is a leverage and distress score, not the paper's safety.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a safer balance sheet than most | `gte 0.5` | soft | 0.25 | illustrative screen, not a published cut-off: half a standard deviation above the session middle; a leverage and distress score. Read with rollup.qmj@v1.profitability_z |

**When the reading lies**

- It leaves out three parts of the published safety score: low beta, low Altman Z and low earnings volatility. Beta lives in rollup.factor_betas@v1.beta_mkt; a name safe here can still swing with the market.
- Null for a negative equity, an untagged debt line, or a bank (debt is its product), and under the same statement-age and 100-name rules as rollup.qmj@v1.profitability_z.
- Don't read a high value as a buy: a safe balance sheet can sit under a collapsing business. Read it with rollup.qmj@v1.profitability_z.

Sources: Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen (2019), Quality Minus Junk, Review of Accounting Studies 24: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11142-018-9470-2

### `rollup.mispricing@v1.composite_inputs`

**How to read it.** How many of the seven anomalies rollup.mispricing@v1.composite_rank averaged for the name: 5, 6 or 7 when the rank is set. A rank on 7 uses every measure; one on 5 leans on fewer. It is the confidence of the rank, not a signal.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a composite rank built on at least six of seven anomalies | `gte 6` | hard | - | illustrative screen: 6 of 7 drops only the least-covered names; the source composite requires all its anomalies, we require 5 |

**When the reading lies**

- A rank averaged over 5 can sit at an extreme by chance. Two names with rank 0.9 are not equally reliable if one has 7 inputs and the other 5. Gate on at least 6 when the rank is the decision.
- Null when the rank is null (fewer than 5 inputs); a hard criterion drops the name.

Sources: Stambaugh, Yu and Yuan (2015), Journal of Finance 70(5)

### `feature.buyback_flag`

**How to read it.** True when the company's share count fell by at least 1% over the year: it bought back more stock than it issued. False means the count held or grew. 0.8% fewer shares is false; 3% fewer is true. It is a flag on feature.shares_change_yoy, measured from share counts, not a repurchase announcement.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| companies shrinking their share count | `eq true` | hard | - | a flag on net share change, not a repurchase announcement |

**When the reading lies**

- Buybacks that only offset stock pay leave the count flat and read false, though cash was spent; a count that fell through a reverse split is not a buyback. Check feature.net_issuance_pct_12m and cash flow.
- Share counts come from filing cover pages, so a count can lag a quarter. Null when the year-ago count is missing or the current one is stale (rollup.fundamentals@v4.market_cap_status STALE); a hard 'eq true' drops those names.
- A company can retire stock while borrowing to do it; check feature.debt_to_equity.

Sources: Ikenberry, Lakonishok and Vermaelen (1995), Market underreaction to open market share repurchases, JFE 39; Pontiff and Woodgate (2008), Share issuance and cross-sectional returns, JF 63

### `feature.market_cap`

**How to read it.** Shares outstanding times the close, in dollars. The usual US buckets: micro under $300M, small $300M to $2B, mid $2B to $10B, large $10B to $200B, mega above $200B. They are conventions, not rules: index providers draw the lines differently and names migrate across them as prices move.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| large cap | `gte 10000000000` | hard | - | - |
| mid cap | `between [2000000000, 10000000000]` | hard | - | - |
| small cap | `between [300000000, 2000000000]` | hard | - | under 300000000 is micro cap; pair with a liquidity gate |

**When the reading lies**

- The count is the company total and the price is this share class's, so a company with two listed classes shows the same cap on both only when they trade at the same price. Check rollup.fundamentals@v4.market_cap_status.
- Null for funds (no share count filed), for a stale count (rollup.fundamentals@v4.market_cap_status STALE) and for a company with no SEC filing (foreign private issuers); a hard size gate drops ETFs and many ADRs.
- Cap is size, not quality or liquidity: a $20B name can trade $5M a day (a controlled company) and a $500M name $50M (a meme). Pair with rollup.price_stats@v2.adv_usd_20d.

Sources: Market-cap bucket conventions: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_capitalization and Morningstar's definitions

### `feature.pe_ratio`

**How to read it.** Close over diluted earnings per share of the trailing twelve months. The long-run market average is around 15 to 20; under 10 is cheap on its face, above 30 is priced for growth. It is only shown when the company earned money, when the figure is recent and when the shares are not an ADR, so a null is common and meaningful.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cheap on trailing earnings | `lt 15` | soft | 3 | with feature.revenue_growth_yoy gte 0 to keep out shrinking businesses; remember loss makers are null, not cheap |
| not priced for perfection | `lte 30` | score | 10 | - |

**When the reading lies**

- Cyclicals look cheapest at the top of their cycle: an oil producer or a chip maker at a P/E of 6 on peak earnings becomes 18 when earnings normalise without the price moving. Pair a low P/E with feature.revenue_growth_yoy and instrument.sector, or compare against several years of earnings (not stored).
- A low P/E is often a value trap: the market has priced in falling earnings, a shrinking industry or heavy debt. A low P/E in feature.trend_state DOWNTREND is the market disagreeing with the ratio.
- One-offs inside the twelve months (an asset sale, a tax gain, a write-down) swing EPS and the ratio; nothing here separates them.
- Null for loss makers (EPS at or below 0), ADRs and stale filings: a hard 'pe_ratio lt 15' silently removes every unprofitable company and every ADR. If that is not the intent, use mode score, or add rollup.financials@v3.financials_status.
- Sector ranges differ by a factor of three (banks and energy low, software high); a single market-wide cut-off mostly selects sectors.

Sources: Problems with taking P/E at face value: https://anurimabanerjee.substack.com/p/problems-with-taking-pe-at-face-value; Cyclical peak earnings and value traps: https://moneyweek.com/10368/the-pitfalls-of-value-investing-53516; Graham and Dodd; Shiller's cyclically adjusted P/E for the multi-year averaging idea

### `feature.revenue_growth_yoy`

**How to read it.** Trailing-twelve-month revenue against the same twelve months a year earlier, as a fraction: 0.10 is 10% growth. Around 0.03 to 0.05 is nominal GDP-like; above 0.15 is a growth company; above 0.30 is rare outside early-stage names; negative is a shrinking business. It lags: the TTM ends at the last filed quarter, which can be four months old.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| growing revenue | `gte 0.1` | soft | 0.03 | - |
| fast growth | `gte 0.25` | soft | 0.05 | check for an acquisition when the jump is sudden |
| not shrinking | `gte 0` | soft | 0.03 | - |

**When the reading lies**

- Acquisitions inflate reported growth and divestitures deflate it; companies report 'organic' growth for that reason and the catalogue has only the reported figure. A jump from 5% to 40% is usually a deal. Check instrument.sic or the company's filings before reading it as demand.
- A tiny year-ago base makes huge percentages (a company going from $10M to $30M is 200%); pair with feature.market_cap or rollup.financials@v3.revenue_ttm.
- Currency moves show up as growth or shrinkage for companies that earn abroad.
- Null with under two years of filings (recent IPOs, foreign filers, funds): a hard criterion rejects them. rollup.financials@v3.financials_status says why.

Sources: Organic versus reported revenue growth, as defined in issuers' filings (e.g. PepsiCo, Moody's 10-K definitions on sec.gov)

### `feature.div_yield`

**How to read it.** Dividends paid in the trailing twelve months over the close, as a fraction: 0.03 is a 3% yield. The S&P 500 yields about 0.013 to 0.02; 0.03 to 0.05 is a solid income name; above 0.08 is where the market is usually pricing a cut (a yield trap: the yield rose because the price fell on the news that the payout cannot last); above 0.10 is a warning, not an opportunity.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| pays a meaningful dividend | `gte 0.03` | soft | 0.005 | - |
| not a yield trap | `lte 0.08` | soft | 0.01 | with feature.trend_state ne DOWNTREND |

**When the reading lies**

- A yield trap looks best right before the cut: a high yield with feature.trend_state DOWNTREND or rollup.price_stats@v2.ret_60d well below 0 is the market expecting the cut. Pair high-yield rules with a trend filter or with earnings coverage (rollup.financials@v3.eps_diluted_ttm above the dividend).
- A special dividend inflates the trailing twelve months for a year: rollup.dividends@v2.div_count_ttm above 4 for a quarterly payer is the sign.
- REITs, MLPs, BDCs and bond or covered-call ETFs yield 0.06 to 0.12 by structure; a market-wide 'above 5%' screen is mostly a list of those. Check instrument.sector and instrument.is_etf.
- Null for a non-payer with too little history to say so; 0 for a known non-payer. A hard 'gte 0.03' drops both, which is usually intended.

Sources: Yield traps and payout ratios (yields above 10% and payouts above 100% precede cuts): https://www.livewiremarkets.com/wires/how-to-avoid-dividend-traps and https://indexes.morningstar.com/insights/perspective/bltdb6d3e043f097825/not-all-dividend-stocks-are-safe-heres-how-to-avoid-dividend-traps

### `rollup.financials@v3.eps_diluted_ttm`

**How to read it.** Diluted earnings per share over the trailing twelve months, summed from the quarters and split-adjusted to the session, in dollars; negative for a loss. The numerator's denominator in feature.pe_ratio, which is null for a loss, so this is the field that can say 'unprofitable': under 0 is a loss-maker, which a growth screen may want and a value screen must know about.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| profitable over the last year | `gt 0` | hard | - | the gate feature.pe_ratio applies implicitly; make it explicit when it matters |
| loss-making (speculative growth, turnarounds) | `lte 0` | hard | - | - |

**When the reading lies**

- The TTM lags: it ends at the last filed quarter, up to four months old (rollup.financials@v3.ttm_as_of); rollup.financials@v3.eps_stale flags a figure more than 480 days old.
- One-offs (a tax gain, a write-down, an asset sale) swing a year's EPS; nothing here separates them from operations.
- Null for funds, foreign filers and companies without four consecutive quarters or a fiscal year on file (rollup.financials@v3.financials_status).

Sources: SEC EDGAR company facts (docs/data/vendors.md)

### `feature.ps_ratio`

**How to read it.** Market capitalisation over revenue of the trailing twelve months: the dollars the market pays for each dollar of yearly sales. Under 1 is cheap on sales (a distressed or low-margin business), under 2 is the value end, 2 to 5 is ordinary, 5 to 10 is a growth price that needs sustained growth to be earned, above 10 prices in years of it. Unlike feature.pe_ratio it exists for a loss maker, which is the point of using it.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cheap on sales | `lt 2` | soft | 0.5 | with feature.net_margin gt 0.05 so a cheap ratio is not just a thin margin, and rollup.financials@v3.financials_status eq OK |
| a growth price (5 to 10 times sales), not a bubble | `between [5, 10]` | soft | 1 | with feature.revenue_growth_yoy gte 0.25: this price is only earned by fast growth |

**When the reading lies**

- Sales are not profit: a grocer and a software company trade at very different P/S for good reason. Pair it with feature.net_margin, or compare within instrument.sector; a low P/S on a 1% margin is not cheap.
- Market cap is the company total count times this class's close (rollup.fundamentals@v4.market_cap_status says whether the count is current), while revenue is the company's: misstated when share classes trade at very different prices, and for an ADR whose ratio is not stored.
- Banks, insurers and other financials report revenue in ways that do not compare with industrials; the ratio means little there.
- Null for funds, for a stale or missing count, for a missing or stale revenue TTM (rollup.financials@v3.financials_status STALE) and for no revenue: a hard 'lt 2' silently drops all of them. The revenue TTM can be four months old.

Sources: Fisher, Super Stocks (1984): the price-to-sales ratio as a valuation screen; O'Shaughnessy, What Works on Wall Street: price-to-sales as the strongest single value ratio in the study; Damodaran, price-to-sales by industry: https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/psdata.html

### `feature.net_margin`

**How to read it.** Net income over revenue of the trailing twelve months, as a fraction: 0.10 is ten cents of profit on each dollar of sales. Under 0 is a loss, 0 to 0.05 is thin (retail, distribution, airlines), 0.10 and above is a healthy business and the line most quality screens draw, 0.20 and above is a franchise (software, payments, a strong brand) that competitors have not been able to compete away.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a quality business | `gte 0.1` | soft | 0.03 | within a sector, not market-wide |
| a franchise margin | `gte 0.2` | soft | 0.03 | - |
| profitable at all | `gt 0` | hard | - | - |

**When the reading lies**

- Margins differ by industry far more than by quality: a 4% grocer is excellent and a 4% software company is broken. Compare within instrument.sector (Damodaran's industry tables show the spread); a market-wide 'above 0.10' mostly selects sectors.
- One-offs (a tax gain, an asset sale, a write-down) move net income and the margin for the twelve months they sit in; a margin far above the company's history is usually one. Pair with rollup.financials@v3.eps_diluted_ttm and its growth feature.eps_growth_yoy.
- Financials report revenue and income in ways that do not compare with industrials; a bank's margin is not an industrial's.
- Null for funds, foreign filers and any company without a revenue or net income TTM (rollup.financials@v3.financials_status); a hard criterion drops them.

Sources: Damodaran, margins by industry: https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/margin.html; Quality screens built on profitability: Novy-Marx (2013), The Other Side of Value: The Gross Profitability Premium

### `feature.payout_ratio`

**How to read it.** Dividends per share of the last 365 days over diluted EPS of the trailing twelve months, as a fraction: 0.5 means half of the earnings is paid out. 0 is a known non-payer, under 0.6 is a payout the earnings cover with room (safe), 0.6 to 1 is stretched (normal for REITs and utilities, which pay out by structure), above 1 pays more than the company earns and is not sustainable. A negative value is a dividend payer with negative earnings: not a negative payout but the least safe case, which a bare 'lt 0.6' would wrongly pass.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a safe dividend | `between [0, 0.6]` | soft | 0.1 | with feature.div_yield gte 0.02 so there is a dividend to be safe about; the lower bound 0 keeps out payers with negative earnings |
| an unsustainable dividend (a cut candidate) | `gt 1.0` | soft | 0.1 | payers with negative earnings are negative, so this misses them: add a second rule 'lt 0' |

**When the reading lies**

- A negative payout means the company pays a dividend while its EPS is negative; 'lt 0.6' passes it. Use 'between 0 and 0.6' for a safe dividend, never a one-sided upper bound.
- Earnings are not cash: REITs, MLPs and capital-heavy businesses pay out of cash flow and show payouts above 1 for years (depreciation depresses EPS). Check instrument.sector before reading 'above 1' as a coming cut; cash-flow coverage is not stored.
- A one-off in the twelve months (a gain, a write-down) swings EPS and the ratio; a payout of 0.2 or 3 for a single year often says that, not the dividend. Compare with feature.eps_growth_yoy.
- Null when the EPS is 0, missing or stale (rollup.financials@v3.eps_stale), for an ADR (EPS per ordinary share, dividend per ADR) and for a payer with too little bar history (rollup.dividends@v2.div_ttm is null): a hard criterion drops all of them.

Sources: Payout ratios above 100% and dividend cuts: https://indexes.morningstar.com/insights/perspective/bltdb6d3e043f097825/not-all-dividend-s; Lintner (1956), Distribution of incomes of corporations among dividends, retained earnings, and taxes: firms target a payout and smooth dividends

### `feature.eps_growth_yoy`

**How to read it.** Diluted EPS of the trailing twelve months against the same twelve months a year earlier, as a fraction: 0.25 is +25%. CAN SLIM asks for annual growth of 25% or more (O'Neil's 'A'); 0.10 to 0.25 is steady, under 0 is a decline, and below -1 means the EPS turned negative. The yearly rate is smoother than feature.eps_growth_qtr_yoy and lags it: compare the two for acceleration.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| annual earnings growth (CAN SLIM A) | `gte 0.25` | soft | 0.05 | with feature.eps_growth_qtr_yoy gte 0.25 for the quarterly half (CAN SLIM C) |
| earnings not shrinking | `gte 0` | soft | 0.03 | - |

**When the reading lies**

- Null when the EPS a year earlier was 0 or negative: a growth rate from a loss or from nothing is meaningless, so a turnaround (a loss to a profit) is null, not infinite. A hard 'gte 0.25' drops every turnaround and every recent IPO; use rollup.financials@v3.eps_diluted_ttm gt 0 to find the profitable ones separately.
- A small year-ago EPS makes huge percentages (from 0.02 to 0.20 is +900%); pair with rollup.financials@v3.eps_diluted_ttm_year_ago gte a few percent of the price.
- One-offs (a tax gain, a write-down) and buybacks (fewer shares lift EPS with no profit growth) move EPS; compare with feature.revenue_growth_yoy and feature.shares_change_yoy.
- Null for funds, foreign filers, fewer than two years of reported quarters or fiscal years, and a stale EPS TTM (rollup.financials@v3.eps_stale).

Sources: O'Neil, How to Make Money in Stocks: CAN SLIM 'A', annual earnings growth of 25% or more; Investor's Business Daily on the CAN SLIM earnings criteria: https://www.investors.com/ibd-university/can-slim/

### `feature.revenue_growth_qtr_yoy`

**How to read it.** Revenue of the latest reported quarter against the same quarter a year earlier, as a fraction: 0.25 is +25%. It is the freshest read of demand the filings give, a quarter nearer than feature.revenue_growth_yoy (which averages four): growth above the yearly rate means it is accelerating, below it that it is slowing. Above 0.25 is fast; negative is shrinking.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| fast quarterly sales growth | `gte 0.25` | soft | 0.05 | with feature.eps_growth_qtr_yoy gte 0.25 |
| quarterly sales not shrinking | `gte 0` | soft | 0.03 | - |

**When the reading lies**

- One quarter is noisy: a timing shift of an order, a 53-week year (its 14-week fourth quarter lifts the rate by about 8%), an acquisition closing or a currency swing moves it; check feature.revenue_growth_yoy points the same way.
- Quarter dates differ by company: the latest quarter ends up to four months before the session, and a company that has not filed its newest quarter shows the previous one (rollup.financials@v3.qtr_as_of says which).
- The fourth quarter is the annual figure minus the nine months, so it is only known once the 10-K is filed and the nine-month figure exists; null otherwise, and null for a quarter older than 480 days (stale). Null for funds, foreign filers and a year-ago quarter that is missing or not positive.

### `feature.eps_growth_qtr_yoy`

**How to read it.** Diluted EPS of the latest reported quarter against the same quarter a year earlier, as a fraction: 0.25 is +25%: CAN SLIM's 'C', current quarterly earnings, which asks for 25% or more (many practitioners want 40% or more for a leader). Compared with feature.eps_growth_yoy: quarterly above the yearly rate is accelerating earnings; below it the growth is fading. A negative value is a decline; below -1 the quarter's EPS turned negative.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| current quarterly earnings growth (CAN SLIM C) | `gte 0.25` | soft | 0.05 | with feature.revenue_growth_qtr_yoy gte 0.25 and feature.eps_growth_yoy gte 0.25 |
| accelerating earnings (a score) | `gte 0.25` | score | 0.25 | accelerating means the quarterly rate is above feature.eps_growth_yoy: read both for the same name; the rule grammar compares to a constant, not to another field |

**When the reading lies**

- Null when the EPS of the quarter a year earlier was 0 or negative: a growth rate from a loss or from nothing is meaningless, so a company that has recently turned profitable is null, not infinite. A hard 'gte 0.25' drops every turnaround; read rollup.financials@v3.eps_diluted_qtr gt 0 for the first profitable quarter.
- One quarter is noisy and one-offs (a tax gain, a write-down, an asset sale) land in a single quarter; confirm with feature.revenue_growth_qtr_yoy (real growth has sales behind it) and feature.eps_growth_yoy.
- A small year-ago EPS gives huge percentages; a fourth quarter is derived (annual minus nine months) and, with share count changes, quarterly EPS is not exactly additive.
- The newest quarter can be four months old (rollup.financials@v3.qtr_as_of); null with no derivable quarter, for a stale quarter, for funds and for foreign filers.

Sources: O'Neil, How to Make Money in Stocks: CAN SLIM 'C', current quarterly earnings per share up 25% or more, preferably accelerating; Investor's Business Daily on the CAN SLIM earnings criteria: https://www.investors.com/ibd-university/can-slim/

### `feature.shares_change_yoy`

**How to read it.** The share count against the count a year earlier, as a fraction: -0.03 is a 3% smaller count. Negative is buybacks (a count that shrinks 1 to 5% a year is a steady repurchaser, returning cash to the holders who stay), around 0 is a stable count, a few percent positive is the ordinary leakage of stock compensation, above 0.10 (10% more shares a year) is dilution: an offering, an acquisition paid in stock or heavy compensation, and a warning for every holder.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| buying back shares | `lte -0.01` | soft | 0.01 | buybacks of 1 to 5% a year are steady; check feature.eps_growth_yoy to see the profit behind them |
| no dilution (a warning above 10% a year) | `lte 0.1` | soft | 0.03 | - |
| diluting (a screen for the warning) | `gt 0.1` | soft | 0.03 | - |

**When the reading lies**

- The counts are the company total from the filings: a cover-page count against a cover-page count, or a weighted average against a weighted average, never one against the other, so a company that began tagging the cover page recently is null. Weighted averages lag a buyback or an offering by about half a period.
- A stock split is adjusted away, but a spin-off, a merger or a reverse merger changes the count without being dilution; check feature.market_cap and the filings when the change is above 0.5.
- A shrinking count can hide an offering: companies buy back what compensation issues, so a flat count is not a company that does nothing. Pair with rollup.financials@v3.eps_diluted_ttm growth.
- Null for funds, companies without two counts a year apart (recent IPOs and filers), and a stale count (rollup.fundamentals@v4.market_cap_status STALE).

Sources: Pontiff and Woodgate (2008), Share Issuance and Cross-Sectional Returns, Journal of Finance: issuers underperform, repurchasers outperform; Faber (2013), Shareholder Yield: the net of dividends and buybacks as the measure of cash returned

**How to read it.** The median analyst price target, in dollars. It is less moved by one extreme target than the consensus, so a gap between the two means a skewed range.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a target exists | `gt 0` | hard | - | - |

**When the reading lies**

- Analysts raise targets after a rise and cut them after a fall, so a target above the price is often the lag of a rally.
- The target is the vendor's current one on the day it was asked, with no date of its own. A stale or revised target is not visible in the number.
- Null with targets_status NOT_COVERED or NOT_FETCHED reads as not covered by our analyst data. The free vendor plan covers few names, and the nightly rotation has not asked every name yet.
- Null with targets_status STALE: the newest snapshot is over 21 days old, so no target is served. rollup.analyst_targets@v1.targets_age_days says how old it is.

### `rollup.analyst_targets@v1.targets_status`

**How to read it.** Whether the price targets of the name can be used. OK: a snapshot at most 21 days old. STALE: older, so no value is served. NOT_COVERED: the vendor has no target for the name. NOT_FETCHED: we have not asked yet.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| targets are current | `eq "OK"` | hard | - | - |
| leave out stale snapshots | `ne "STALE"` | hard | - | - |

**When the reading lies**

- NOT_COVERED and NOT_FETCHED read the same to a trader, as not covered. The admin cause tells them apart, and NOT_FETCHED ends as the nightly rotation reaches the name.
- STALE is not the old value: a snapshot past 21 days serves nothing, so a vendor outage shows as STALE rather than as old numbers.

### `rollup.mispricing@v1.composite_rank`

**How to read it.** The composite mispricing rank, 0 to 1. It is the mean percentile rank of seven anomalies: net share issuance, accruals, net operating assets, asset growth, a simplified Ohlson O-score, and inverted gross profitability and return on assets. Each is oriented so that high means overpriced. 1 is the most overpriced name of the session, 0 the most underpriced; 0.5 is the middle. Combining many noisy anomalies predicts returns better than any one, and most of the spread in the literature sits on the overpriced end.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| the most underpriced fifth (the long leg) | `lte 0.2` | hard | - | null (too few anomalies known) never passes |
| the most overpriced fifth (the short leg, or an avoid list) | `gte 0.8` | hard | - | - |

**When the reading lies**

- It is not Stambaugh, Yu and Yuan's composite: four of their eleven anomalies are missing. Composite equity issuance and investment-to-assets need cash-flow detail we do not store. Financial distress needs returns and volatility. Momentum lives in the price fields. rollup.mispricing@v1.composite_inputs says how many of the seven an instrument's rank averages; a rank on five is less reliable than one on seven.
- Missing is left out, never filled. An anomaly a company cannot report (an untagged debt line, no gross profit) drops out of its own mean. Fewer than five of seven gives null. A hard criterion therefore drops those names rather than passing them.
- A rank is relative to the session's cross-section, so it moves when other names move and carries no level. It uses all instruments with statements, across industries and sizes; banks, brokers and insurers have most inputs null and are usually unranked.
- Statement figures enter from the session after SEC acceptance of the filing. A rank can be up to four months stale for a company that has not filed lately. rollup.balance_sheet@v2.bs_as_of shows how old the balance sheet is. Individual anomalies have decayed since publication (accruals especially); the composite holds up better, and it is also the benchmark any other cross-sectional edge has to beat.

Sources: Stambaugh, Yu and Yuan (2015), Journal of Finance 70(5); docs/edge-ideas/altdata-text-ml.md (composite_mispricing_score)

### `rollup.mispricing@v1.quality_score`

**How to read it.** A quality score from 0 to 1. It is the mean percentile rank of six measures among instruments with a value that session. The measures are return on equity, return on assets, gross margin, net margin, operating cash flow over total assets and low debt to equity. 0.8 is better than about 80% of the cross-section on the average measure. Quality names are profitable, cash-generating and conservatively financed; the idea is to hold momentum within them so the junk that drives momentum crashes is excluded.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a high-quality name | `gte 0.7` | soft | 0.1 x the threshold | - |

**When the reading lies**

- QMJ-like, not QMJ. It omits Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen's growth (five-year changes), payout (buybacks, dividends, accruals) and low-beta parts. It uses one year of data with no stability measure. It ranks across industries, so an asset-light software firm outranks a utility on margins. It weights the six measures equally.
- Needs at least four of the six measures. Gross margin is missing for the many filers that do not tag GrossProfit. Debt to equity is missing for those with negative equity or untagged debt. A score therefore rests on different measures for different names. Missing is dropped, never counted as zero; a hard criterion drops names with too few.
- Banks and insurers are scored on the measures they report (return on equity and assets, net margin) and their debt to equity is not comparable; read them separately.
- Statement figures enter from the session after SEC acceptance of the filing and can be four months old; the score is relative to the session's cross-section and has no level.

Sources: Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen (2019), Review of Accounting Studies 24(1), Quality Minus Junk; docs/edge-ideas/cross-sectional.md (momentum_x_quality_interaction)

### `rollup.mispricing@v1.asset_growth`

**How to read it.** Growth of total assets over the year to the latest balance sheet: total assets over the same line a year earlier, less one (0.10 is +10%). Firms that expand their balance sheet fast, by acquisition, capex or hoarding, tend to earn lower returns afterwards (Cooper, Gulen and Schill), so high growth counts as overpriced in rollup.mispricing@v1.composite_rank.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| assets not growing fast | `lte 0.2` | soft | 0.5 x the threshold | - |

**When the reading lies**

- An acquisition or a financing round can double assets overnight without any organic growth; nothing here separates them. Check rollup.balance_sheet@v2.total_assets and the filings.
- The year-ago figure is the balance sheet ending within 40 days of the latest one's date minus 365 days, as restated by any filing accepted by the session. A company with less than a year of filings, or a gap, is null.
- Rate of change of a size measure: a small company swings more than a large one. Banks and insurers grow assets by lending and are not comparable.

Sources: Cooper, Gulen and Schill (2008), Journal of Finance 63(4), Asset Growth and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns

### `rollup.mispricing@v1.accruals`

**How to read it.** Accruals as a share of total assets: net income minus operating cash flow, both over the trailing twelve months, over total assets at the latest balance sheet. Positive means reported earnings exceed the cash behind them (accounting profit not yet realised), negative means cash exceeds earnings. Sloan found high accruals followed by low returns.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| earnings backed by cash | `lte 0` | soft | 0.5 x the threshold | - |

**When the reading lies**

- A weak signal on its own: the accruals anomaly has largely vanished in US large caps since about 2002 and is subsumed by cash-based profitability. It is here as one input of rollup.mispricing@v1.composite_rank, not a screen to trade alone.
- Uses the latest total assets, not the average of two years as Sloan did, and the whole income-less-cash gap, not only working-capital accruals; depreciation and large non-cash items move it.
- Net income and operating cash flow come from different groups: check rollup.cash_flow@v2.cf_as_of and rollup.financials@v3.ttm_as_of that they end on the same quarter. Null for banks, brokers and insurers, whose cash flows are not comparable.

Sources: Sloan (1996), The Accounting Review 71(3), Do Stock Prices Fully Reflect Information in Accruals and Cash Flows about Future Earnings

### `rollup.mispricing@v1.gross_profit_to_assets`

**How to read it.** Gross profitability: gross profit over the trailing twelve months over total assets at the latest balance sheet (0.30 is 30 cents of gross profit a year per dollar of assets). Novy-Marx found it predicts returns about as well as value, and it hedges value; high is underpriced, so it enters rollup.mispricing@v1.composite_rank inverted.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a high gross profit on assets | `gte 0.3` | soft | 0.2 x the threshold | - |

**When the reading lies**

- Null for the many filers that do not tag GrossProfit, and null is not zero: a hard criterion drops them. Filers draw the line between cost of revenue and operating costs differently, so compare within an industry.
- Asset-heavy businesses score low and asset-light ones high regardless of quality; intangible assets that are not on the balance sheet (brands, software) understate the base.
- Null for banks, brokers and insurers.

Sources: Novy-Marx (2013), Journal of Financial Economics 108(1), The Other Side of Value: The Gross Profitability Premium

### `rollup.mispricing@v1.net_operating_assets`

**How to read it.** Net operating assets as a share of total assets. Operating assets are assets less cash; operating liabilities are assets less debt and equity. The difference is debt plus equity less cash, divided by total assets. A high value means the business has tied up much capital in operations beyond what operating liabilities fund. Hirshleifer, Hou, Teoh and Zhang link that to accumulated accruals and low future returns.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a lean balance sheet | `lte 1` | soft | 0.2 x the threshold | - |

**When the reading lies**

- Short-term investments, minority interest and preferred stock are not separated: investments count as operating, so a company holding treasuries looks asset-heavy. The base is the latest total assets, not the year-ago assets of the paper.
- Null when total_debt is null (an untagged debt line is not zero) and for banks, brokers and insurers, whose balance sheets are not operating ones.
- Moves slowly and with every acquisition; a negative value (net cash larger than the capital employed) is possible and valid.

Sources: Hirshleifer, Hou, Teoh and Zhang (2004), Journal of Accounting and Economics 38, Do Investors Overvalue Firms with Bloated Balance Sheets?

### `rollup.mispricing@v1.ohlson_o`

**How to read it.** A simplified Ohlson O-score, a bankruptcy-risk index from size, leverage, working capital, current ratio, a negative-equity flag, profitability and cash flow. Higher is riskier; it is a rank input to rollup.mispricing@v1.composite_rank, not a probability and not comparable with the paper's levels.

**The criterion per intent**

| Intent | Criterion | Mode | Near-miss band | How to combine it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a low bankruptcy-risk rank input (compare within one session) | `lte -3` | soft | 0.3 x the threshold | - |

**When the reading lies**

- Two of Ohlson's terms are omitted because they need last year's net income, which no group stores. They are a loss in both of the last two years and the change in net income. The GNP price-index deflator on the size term is omitted too. The deflator is a constant within a session, so ranks are unaffected, but the level shifts across sessions: compare only within one session.
- Operating cash flow stands in for funds from operations, and total assets are in dollars, not thousands of 1968 dollars; both only shift or rescale the score.
- Null for banks, brokers and insurers, for a filer missing current assets, current liabilities, total liabilities or operating cash flow, and when a statement is stale.

Sources: Ohlson (1980), Journal of Accounting Research 18(1), Financial Ratios and the Probabilistic Prediction of Bankruptcy
