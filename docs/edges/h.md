## BDC price to NAV reversion (`bdc_price_to_nav_reversion`)

**Status:** candidate. **Family:** H. **Thesis:** Business development companies trading at a deep discount to their last reported net asset value outperform those at premiums over the next quarter.

- **Outcome:** excess return, over 60 sessions, vs SPY, entered 1 session after the decision session, costs 30 bps
- **Schedule:** month end
- **Universe:** preset `bdc_listed`
- **Top K:** 10
- **Screeners:** `bdc_price_to_nav`
- **Baselines:** none yet
- **Frozen period:** from 2026-04-01
- **Notes:** Month-end start sessions spaced at least 60 sessions apart (one quarter, the NAV cadence). The NAV is not a new source: the net asset value per share of a BDC is its stockholders' equity over its shares, so price to NAV is pb_ratio of the stored statements (a BDC's equity is its net assets), public from its 10-Q or 10-K acceptance (statements_accepted, H2). D5's events/fund_nav (Tiingo NAV series) does not carry BDCs, whose NAV is quarterly. The universe is a hand list of listed BDCs keyed by instrument id (resolved from the tickers through the SymbolResolver; the reference has no BDC type and the SEC company table no SIC for them), kept equal in the selection preset and the screener by a fitness test. History before the frozen period is survivor-only: BDCs merged away or delisted before the list was made are missing, and renamed ones (ORCC to OBDC 2023, AINV to MFIC 2022) are resolved by id. The book value is the balance sheet and the share count the newest cover page, which can be a quarter newer, so price to NAV mixes two dates; the screener also requires the balance sheet to be at most 135 days old (rollup.qmj@v1.balance_sheet_age_days), because pb_ratio refuses only a 480-day-old one. About 38 names: 10 picks is a quarter of the list, so the decile spread is over a handful of names a session and the harness's t is weak; the catalogue's rule (discount against the name's own three-year average and peers) needs a NAV history that is not stored, so v1 ranks the plain price to NAV, and says so. The discount must be read with the NAV trend (a falling NAV earns its discount): not conditioned in v1.

**Quality bar**

1. **Mechanism:** BDC holders are retail and buy on yield and sell on dividend cuts; there is no institutional arbitrage because BDCs are excluded from most indexes (the acquired-fund-fee rule drove them out of the S&P and Russell indexes in 2014) and are hard to borrow, and the net asset value is published only quarterly, so the price drifts away from it.
2. **Persistence:** Index exclusion and the 2014 rule are structural, the quarterly NAV cadence is regulatory and the sector is small (about 50 listed BDCs, about 70 billion dollars), too small for large funds: the closed-end fund literature finds discounts predict returns (Pontiff 1995), and the one cited BDC datum is that the discount move dominated a year of price and NAV returns.
3. **Outcome:** Excess return over SPY over the 60 sessions (one quarter) after the entry close (the session after a month-end decision), net of 30 bps a round trip (an assumption for small, wide-spread names).
4. **Trigger timing:** Every statement figure is read as of the session its filing's acceptance was public (statements_accepted, H2), never at its period end, so the month-end decision session D sees only filings accepted by D. The entry session is S = D + 1 (start_offset_sessions = 1): the first fill is the next session's close and the window runs from S's close; the screen is read at D, the outcome at S. The NAV used is the last quarterly report accepted by D, up to four months old; the price is D's close.
5. **Faithful replication:** No canonical academic rule. The practitioner metric is Closed-End Fund Advisors' three-year relative discount (the current discount against the name's own trailing three-year average); this catalogue's formalisation ranks by price to NAV relative to the name and to peers and holds the bottom quintile, rebalanced quarterly after the 10-Q. v1 is the simpler plain price to NAV on the hand list, top 10 lowest, with the omitted history named in the notes: a partial replication of a rule that is itself a formalisation.
6. **Expected size and sample:** Closed-end funds with a 20% discount earned about 6% a year more over the next 12 months (Pontiff 1995); a systematic CEF discount strategy earned 18.2% a year gross long-short, Sharpe 1.9, 582% turnover, 1984-2011 (Patro, Piccotti and Wu 2017, via CXO Advisory). No BDC-specific published figure exists, so there is no published bar. About 38 names and a handful of picks: the harness's t will be wide, and it reports the sessions beside it.
7. **Capacity and costs:** Long-only, 20 thousand to 200 thousand dollars a name; BDC dividends are large (ordinary income and qualified-dividend mix); shorting premium BDCs is impractical (borrow, dividend owed). The 30 bps round trip is the assumption.
8. **Failure modes and retirement:** Discounts cluster in credit-quality losers: a deep discount on a falling NAV is earned, not an opportunity (rising non-accruals, a dividend cut), and the NAV can be marked up or down only quarterly. Floating-rate assets give interest-rate beta and a small-cap and value exposure. Retire if the frozen-period decile spread is not above zero or the picks do not beat the buy-and-hold mean of the listed BDCs.
9. **Decoys:** The sector's interest-rate and credit beta (the buy-and-hold mean of the listed BDCs is the base), small-cap and value exposure (the size baseline is not run on the hand list), and credit-quality losers (compare the picks' NAV change).

## Dual-class share spread reversion (`dual_class_spread_reversion`)

**Status:** candidate. **Family:** H. **Thesis:** When one share class of a company trades much cheaper than its sibling class against their own history, the spread narrows: the cheap class beats the rich one over the next one to three months.

- **Outcome:** pair spread, over 20, 60 sessions, entered 1 session after the decision session, the cheap share class against its sibling from rollup.share_classes@v1.sibling_id, short leg pays borrow, costs 10 bps
- **Schedule:** month end
- **Universe:** `instrument.security_type in ('COMMON_STOCK', 'ADR')` and `instrument.status eq 'ACTIVE'` and `rollup.price_stats@v2.close gt 3` and `rollup.price_stats@v2.adv_usd_20d gte 2000000` and `rollup.share_classes@v1.sibling_status eq 'PAIRED'` and `rollup.share_classes@v1.spread_z_252d gte -20`
- **Top K:** 5
- **Screeners:** `dual_class_cheap_leg`
- **Baselines:** `momentum_12_1`, `size_small`
- **Frozen period:** from 2026-04-01
- **Notes:** The outcome is the pair_spread of EB H9: a pick is the cheap class of a pair, and its value is its return over the horizon less its sibling's, less two round trips and the borrow on the sibling (the rich leg); a hit is a positive value. It is a two-leg spread, so the result is the spread and nothing is filed under [published] (Schultz and Shive's figures are for trades opened at a fixed spread and held to closure, not this monthly rule). The pair is read from share_classes@v1 at D (the issuer's two classes of the reference snapshot the session sees, joined on CIK and share-class FIGI, never a hand list and never today's reference): only issuers with exactly two listed classes are PAIRED, three or more are AMBIGUOUS and no pair is guessed, and the universe requires PAIRED, so both classes must pass the liquidity floors (a pair with one class below $3 or 2M dollars a day is out). Declared thresholds, not tuned: the cheap class is one at or below -2 standard deviations of its own log price ratio over the last 252 sessions (spread_z_252d), at least 200 sessions of both bars needed; the stored bars start 2024-10, so the first z-scores are about a year old. The base is every eligible class against its sibling (a random side of the pair), so the lift is over the pair's own noise. Few pairs (about 15 to 25 US issuers) and few below -2 at any month end: the harness reports the picks and the sessions beside each number, and most sessions will pick none. Proxies, each stated: a z-score of the ratio against its own year, not Schultz and Shive's deviation from a moving average; no dividend or voting parity check (a class with different dividend or voting rights holds a gap for years: the z-score then measures drift inside the gap); the entry and exit are month-end closes with no convergence exit; the borrow is the site's default, not a quote. Month-end start sessions at least 20 sessions apart.

**Quality bar**

1. **Mechanism:** Two classes with the same cash flows should keep a stable price ratio, but index membership, option hedging and retail flow hit one ticker more than the other, and nobody is paid to arbitrage a one to three percent spread that closes slowly and costs borrow to hold (Schultz and Shive 2010; Froot and Dabora 1999).
2. **Persistence:** Capacity is tens of pairs and the carry is negative for arbitrageurs, so capital stays away; but Froot and Dabora's twins deviated 10 to 30 percent for years, so a spread can keep widening, and collapses of a class structure deliver it in one jump. Persistence is a claim to test.
3. **Outcome:** The cheap class's return less its sibling's over 20 and 60 sessions after the entry close, net of two round trips at 10 bps and the borrow on the sibling: a hit is a positive spread.
4. **Trigger timing:** The pair and the z-score are read at the decision session D (a month end) from the closes through D and the reference snapshot D sees, so a reclassification counts from its own date; the entry session is S = D + 1 (start_offset_sessions = 1); both legs are valued from the same stored outcome rows at S.
5. **Faithful replication:** Schultz and Shive: when the relative price deviation of two classes exceeds a threshold against its moving average, long the cheap and short the rich class, exit on reversion. Here: the cheap class of a paired issuer at 2 or more standard deviations under its own 252-session ratio, held 20 and 60 sessions, the sibling shorted, the pick limit 5 pairs a month.
6. **Expected size and sample:** Schultz and Shive report that 82 percent of 3,687 trades opened at a 1 percent spread and held to closure were profitable, and 9.75 percent on average over 213 days for spreads above 10 percent, 100 US pairs 1993-2006 (verified through Alpha Architect's summary of the paper); those are trades held to closure, not this rule. Recent US spreads are one to two percent and narrowing (GOOG and GOOGL), so expect little, in very few pairs; the history holds a year of month-ends, under the 40 the bar asks.
7. **Capacity and costs:** Tiny: capacity of tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars a pair on the liquid pairs, which is why large funds do not trade it. Two legs pay two round trips (the 10 bps is the assumption) and the borrow on the rich class, cheap on mega-caps; the convergence date is unknown, so the carry can be negative.
8. **Failure modes and retirement:** Control events legitimately widen a spread (a takeover rumour, a proxy fight, a recapitalisation), a class with different voting or dividend rights keeps a gap for years, and a spread can widen for longer than the horizon. Retire if the frozen-period mean spread of the picks is not above the mean of every eligible class against its sibling, or the sample stays under a handful of picks after the 40 months.
9. **Decoys:** The liquidity premium of the thinner class, voting premia around control events, and index membership of one class: the 12-1 momentum and size baselines on the same sessions (stock rankings, so a rough read for a pair); the base here is every class against its sibling.

**Sources**

- Lamont, O. and Thaler, R., Can the Market Add and Subtract? Mispricing in Tech Stock Carve-outs, Journal of Political Economy, 2003 <https://www.nber.org/papers/w8302>

9. **Decoys:** Dollar beta and risk-on equity beta; the benchmark excludes the dollar fund on purpose (a held UUP is a bet against the foreign mix).

3. **Outcome:** The book's return over the 21 sessions from the close after each month end, less the near-equal mix of the three funds held always, net of the cost on the weight change; a fund with a falling trend is held as cash. 5. **Faithful replication:** The source's rule is a futures-curve trigger on the single ETP (Erb and Harvey 2006; Gorton, Hayashi and Rouwenhorst 2013). 6. **Expected size and sample:** Guedj, Li and McCann (2011) document the roll losses in UNG and USO; no recent split is on file. The size of a trend-filtered avoidance is not published.
7. **Capacity and costs:** Capacity is small to moderate in three funds; a few switches a year; cost_bps 20 assumed. The source's short side needs borrow (1 to 5 percent on UNG, not verified): not charged.
8. **Failure modes and retirement:** A cold-snap rally while the book is in cash costs a month against the held mix; the result rests on three funds and a few years of falling prices. Retire if the frozen-period picks-series t is under 2.
9. **Decoys:** The energy market's own direction: in the funds' falling years cash looks good by construction. Read it beside commodity_roll_momentum's energy funds.

## Retail lottery-option attention (`retail_options_lottery_attention`)

**Status:** candidate. **Family:** H. **Thesis:** Lottery-like stocks with heavy option volume against their share volume, the names retail traders buy short-dated calls in, underperform over the next weeks.

- **Outcome:** excess return, over 6, 20 sessions, vs SPY, entered 1 session after the decision session, costs 10 bps
- **Schedule:** every session
- **Universe:** preset `liquid_common_stocks`

- **Baselines:** `momentum_12_1`, `size_small`
- **Frozen period:** from 2026-04-01
- **Notes:** The papers' feature, the share of option volume in short-dated out-of-the-money calls (lottery_call_share), and a meme score are not stored: the screener is a proxy, names with a best day of 10% or more in the last 21 sessions (max_ret_21d, a pre-declared threshold, not tuned) ranked by the option-to-stock volume ratio, the highest first. Chain flow is stored from 2026-10-02 only: about 11 sessions by 2026-10-16, none before the frozen start 2026-04-01, so no verdict is possible for a long time; the harness reports the count. **Quality bar**

1. **Mechanism:** Retail traders buy short-dated out-of-the-money calls through wholesalers; market makers hedge by buying the stock, which lifts it transiently, and the pressure reverses; the calls are overpriced (Byun and Kim 2016; Bryzgalova, Pavlova and Sikorskaya 2023).
2. **Persistence:** Retail's preference for skewness is stable and zero-commission apps gamify it; the wholesalers internalise the flow, so institutions see it only indirectly, and shorting these names is costly and squeeze-prone, which keeps the overpricing from being traded away. Capacity per name is small.
3. **Outcome:** The short's value: minus the excess return over SPY for 6 and 20 sessions after the entry close, less 10 bps a round trip and the assumed borrow cost.
4. **Trigger timing:** The best day of the last 21 sessions and the option-to-stock volume ratio are computed from the close and the chain of the decision session D; the entry session is S = D + 1. A name with no chain, no option volume or no 21-session history has no value and is not ranked.
5. **Faithful replication:** Byun and Kim: calls with lottery features (high IV, out of the money, short maturity) earn significantly lower returns; Bryzgalova et al.: retail identified by wholesaler price improvement. Neither is stored: the proxy ranks lottery-like names (best day of 10% or more) by option volume over stock volume, the 30 highest.
6. **Expected size and sample:** No stock-return figure is verified for the proxy. The papers report retail losing money in options in aggregate (the exact figure is not verified) and negative subsequent returns after lottery demand; the harness reports t and the sessions, and with a short chain history it will say so.
7. **Capacity and costs:** An individual cannot short the microcaps where this effect lives: the short is held only among names above the capacity floor, with the assumed borrow cost, and shorting squeeze-prone names (GME, AMC) is the dangerous trade. Treat the list as an avoid list or a call-selling selector, not a short book.
8. **Failure modes and retirement:** Squeezes, and a best day that was news (a bid or a report) rather than lottery demand. Retire if the frozen-period short value is not above zero after borrow, or the picks do not do worse than the buy-and-hold mean of the eligible names.
9. **Decoys:** Short-term reversal, MAX and idiosyncratic volatility (the same lottery cluster: lottery_max is its screen), momentum and size: the 12-1 momentum and size baselines on the same sessions.

**Status:** candidate. **Family:** H. **Thesis:** Stocks that reverse-split to cure a sub-$1 bid price underperform for one to three years, so they belong on an exclusion list.

- **Outcome:** excess return, over 250, 500 sessions, vs SPY, starting +1 sessions from the event, costs 20 bps
- **Schedule:** on each `reverse_split` (a reverse split (reverse_splits@v1; ADR 0075); anchor: the ex-date; funds are left out by the edge's universe)
- **Universe:** `instrument.security_type eq 'COMMON_STOCK'` and `instrument.status eq 'ACTIVE'` and `rollup.price_stats@v2.adv_usd_20d gte 5000000`
- **Top K:** 20
- **Screeners:** `reverse_split_recent`
- **Baselines:** `size_small`
- **Frozen period:** from 2026-04-01
- **Notes:** Short side, owner decisions O1 and O2 (2026-10-17): a short result on liquid names may be a verdict basis; the outcome is minus the excess return over SPY, less cost and the site's assumed borrow (100 bps a year; no borrow data stored). The idea's own form is a filter (leave reverse splitters out of a long screen); the harness tests it as the short side, which is the same claim stated so that a negative drift is a pass. The anchor is the split's ex-date (reverse_splits@v1, event class reverse_split, ADR 0075): the decision session D is the ex-date and the entry session S is the next one (start_offset_sessions = 1). The events table has no known_from, so a split is read from its ex-date on, which is later than its announcement and so never early. Only splits with a ratio below 1 count; the fund universe is left out by this document's COMMON_STOCK filter. THE HISTORY LIMITS stand: most reverse splitters are microcaps, so the liquid-name floor (20-day dollar volume of $5M) leaves few events and the verdict rests on a small count; the assumed 100 bps a year understates the real borrow of such a name (20 to 100 percent a year, often no borrow at all), so a short result here is the best case for the short; and the 250 and 500-session horizons outlast the frozen period for any split since 2026-04-01, so only a split from before it fills a frozen slice, and a 500-session outcome exists only for splits that long ago (stored outcomes reach 2018 after the D0 backfill). Every eligible name is the base and the picks are the flagged names ranked by dollar volume (the most liquid shorted first), so the lift reads the flagged names against all eligible names, not against themselves. A second reverse split of the same name within 250 sessions is counted once per decision session, as the harness dedupes. Trial count: one screener, two horizons.

**Quality bar**

1. **Mechanism:** Compliance-driven splits signal distress, continued dilution (at-the-market offerings resume once the price is above $1) and index and retail abandonment; Nasdaq rule changes in 2024 and 2025 tightened cures for repeat offenders.
2. **Persistence:** Firms must split or delist and the dilution machine is the business model of many micro-cap issuers; shorting is costly, so prices stay too high (Kim, Klein and Rosenfeld 2008, exact figure not verified).
3. **Outcome:** Minus the excess return over SPY over 250 and 500 sessions after the entry close (the session after the split's ex-date), net of 20 bps a round trip and the assumed borrow.
4. **Trigger timing:** The split is known from its ex-date (the anchor and the decision session D); the entry session S = D + 1 (start_offset_sessions = 1). The names are read at D from reverse_splits@v1 (sessions_since_reverse_split = 0), built only from splits with ts <= D, never from one that goes ex after D's close. The events table has no known_from, so the ex-date is the earliest the split is read, and a late-stored split is read late, never early.
5. **Faithful replication:** The source's rule first: the three-year abnormal return after a reverse split (Kim, Klein and Rosenfeld 2008). Here reverse_split_recent v1 takes the liquid common stocks with a reverse split of the last 20 sessions and the harness shorts the 20 most liquid for 250 and 500 sessions.
6. **Expected size and sample:** Kim, Klein and Rosenfeld (2008) report negative long-run abnormal returns after a reverse split and the page puts them at about -15 to -20 percent over three years, an exact figure it marks as not verified, so no published figure is set. Liquid reverse splitters are few; independent events are the binding count, and the 500-session outcome needs history the store reaches only from 2018.
7. **Capacity and costs:** Liquid splitters are shorted at the borrow of a distressed name, often 20 to 100 percent a year or none at all; the outcome deducts 20 bps and the site's assumed 100 bps a year, so the result is a best case. The usable form for an individual is the exclusion list for a long screen, which costs nothing.
8. **Failure modes and retirement:** A compliance split that works (the price holds above $1 and the business recovers); a split that goes with a merger or a spin-off (ratio and ex-date read the same); the 2024 and 2025 Nasdaq rule changes, which may have changed the cycle; heavy overlap with distress, low price and share issuance. Retire if the frozen-period short return of the picks is not above the base rate of every eligible name.
9. **Decoys:** Distress, low price and high share issuance (the net_share_issuance and distress edges) and size: the size baseline. A reverse split is the visible end of a long slide, so part of the drift was already in the price.

## Tender-offer drift after expiry (`tender_offer_drift`)

**Status:** candidate. **Family:** H. **Thesis:** Stocks whose own tender offer for their shares has run its course outperform the market for a year or more afterwards.

- **Outcome:** excess return, over 60, 250 sessions, vs SPY, starting +21 sessions from the event, costs 20 bps
- **Schedule:** on each `tender_offer` (an SC TO-I issuer or SC TO-T third-party tender offer (filing_events@v1); anchor: the session it became known)
- **Universe:** `instrument.security_type eq 'COMMON_STOCK'` and `instrument.status eq 'ACTIVE'` and `rollup.price_stats@v2.close gt 5` and `rollup.price_stats@v2.adv_usd_20d gte 5000000`
- **Top K:** 10
- **Screeners:** `tender_offer_filed`
- **Baselines:** `size_small`
- **Frozen period:** from 2026-04-01
- **Notes:** The anchor is the session the SC TO-I or SC TO-T filing became known (the first session whose close follows its acceptance), not the expiry: the expiry is not stored. A tender offer must stay open at least 20 business days (SEC Rule 14e-1), so the entry is 21 sessions after the anchor (start_offset_sessions = 21; the decision session D is 20 sessions on), the earliest the offer can have expired; a longer or extended offer enters before expiry, and the harness cannot tell. That approximates the paper's "first close after expiry" and is the main limit of this edge. The class joins SC TO-I (the issuer's own offer) and SC TO-T (a third party's, a takeover): a third-party offer is a different event, and its target usually delists, so the issuer-only split is a follow-up (a new column of filing_events). The 250-session horizon outlasts the frozen period for any event filed since 2026-04-01, so only the 60-session read has a frozen slice for a long while. Filings coverage has a documented null history (decision log 2026-10-18); about 20 to 40 events a year make the independent count small.

**Quality bar**

1. **Mechanism:** Management signals undervaluation with its own money; arbitrageurs who bought for the tender sell the pro-rated remainder at expiry, which makes the post-expiry dip the cheap entry; long-horizon investors are slow to re-rate (Lakonishok and Vermaelen 1990; Peyer and Vermaelen 2009).
2. **Persistence:** The events are small-cap and infrequent (about 20 to 40 US self-tenders a year), so funds ignore them; Peyer and Vermaelen (2009) show the anomaly survived its 1990 publication. Tender-specific evidence after 2010 is not on file, and the open-market buyback anomaly largely faded after 2003 (Fu and Huang 2016), so persistence is a claim to test.
3. **Outcome:** Excess return over SPY over 60 and 250 sessions after the entry close, 21 sessions after the tender offer became known (about when it can first have expired), net of 20 bps a round trip: a long hold after the offer.
4. **Trigger timing:** The filing is known from the first session whose close follows its SEC acceptance (the anchor); the entry session S = anchor + 21 (start_offset_sessions = 21) and the decision session D = S - 1, 20 sessions after the anchor. The names are read at D from filing_events@v1 (sessions_since_tender_offer = 20), a filing the session had not yet seen is never read. The expiry itself is not read: a tender offer open at least 20 business days has expired by S only if it was not extended.
5. **Faithful replication:** The source's rule first: buy at the first close after expiry and hold two years (Lakonishok and Vermaelen 1990). Here tender_offer_filed v1 takes the liquid names with a tender offer 20 or more sessions old and the harness holds the 10 most liquid for 60 and 250 sessions (the 2-year hold exceeds the stored outcome history).
6. **Expected size and sample:** Abnormal returns of about 9% for the full sample and about 23% for small firms after expiry (Lakonishok and Vermaelen 1990; the page marks the figure as not verified, so no published figure is set); post-2010 tender-specific evidence is not on file, and the page's own assumption is that the effect has halved. Roughly 20 to 40 events a year: independent events are the binding count, and 250-session outcomes need history the store reaches only from 2018.
7. **Capacity and costs:** Small and mid caps, long only, with a capacity of $20k to $100k a name on the page's estimate; the filter here (5M dollars a day) keeps only the liquid end, which is not where the largest drift sat. The outcome deducts 20 bps a round trip.
8. **Failure modes and retirement:** A third-party tender offer (a takeover) delists the target and is not the issuer self-tender the thesis is about; an extended offer is entered early; the buyback anomaly faded after 2003. Retire if the frozen-period excess return of the picks is not above the base rate of every eligible name.
9. **Decoys:** Size and value (tender names are small and cheap) and the reversal of a price drop that prompted the offer: the size baseline. Overlaps any buyback idea (variant: tender only).

**Sources**

- Lakonishok, J. and Vermaelen, T., Anomalous price behavior around repurchase tender offers, Journal of Finance, 1990 <https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v45y1990i2p455-77.html>
- Peyer, U. and Vermaelen, T., The nature and persistence of buyback anomalies, Review of Financial Studies, 2009 <https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v22y2009i4p1693-1745.html>
- Fu, F. and Huang, S., The persistence of long-run abnormal returns following stock repurchases and offerings, Management Science, 2016 <https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v62y2016i4p964-984.html>
- Ikenberry, D., Lakonishok, J. and Vermaelen, T. (1995), Market underreaction to open market share repurchases, Journal of Financial Economics <https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v39y1995i2-3p181-208.html>

## Treasury funds before month end (`treasury_month_end_extension`)

**Status:** candidate. **Family:** H. **Thesis:** Treasury and aggregate bond funds earn more over the last three sessions of a month than over the same number of sessions elsewhere.

- **Outcome:** excess return, over 3 sessions, entered 1 session after the decision session
- **Schedule:** calendar:pre month end
- **Universe:** `instrument.symbol in ('TLT', 'IEF', 'AGG')`
- **Top K:** all qualified names
- **Screeners:** none yet
- **Baselines:** none yet
- **Frozen period:** from 2026-04-01
- **Notes:** VERDICTS ARE UNMEASURED until the D0 Tiingo outcomes backfill (due 2026-11-09): the stored outcome history of the three funds is too short to measure a window edge before it. The window is the calendar's pre_month_end (core/time/windows.py, ADR 0075): the last 3 sessions of every month; the entry session S is the session before the first of them and the decision session D the one before S, so the value is the fund's raw return over the month's last 3 sessions from the close of the session before. It is compared with the fund's own non-overlapping 3-session stretches outside every window (ADR 0073), and the harness pairs each window with the stretch right after it so that a rising fund does not pass on its drift. TLT, IEF and AGG are three trials, not one (deflated Sharpe ratio). The source holds the funds for 2 to 3 sessions up to the first session after month end; the window here stops at the month's last session (the closed set of windows has no window that crosses the month end), and it is distinct from the turn_of_month window of the equity edge (the month's last session and the 3 after it), so the two edges share at most one session. The equity leg of the source (sell equities into month end) is the turn-of-month edge's opposite and is not filed. Schwarz's Sharpe near 1 after costs is from a search summary the page did not read, so no [published] figure is set; no cost is deducted from a timed position in this version (ADR 0073). About 12 windows a year, so about 100 since 2018.

**Quality bar**

1. **Mechanism:** Bond index duration jumps at month end when new issues enter the indexes and short bonds drop out, so passive trackers must buy duration on a known date; pensions and insurers also raise cash by selling equities before month-end payments and park it in bonds (Etula, Rinne, Suominen and Vaittinen 2020).
2. **Persistence:** Index rules are public and mechanical and the flow is non-discretionary, which is why it can persist; Etula et al show it for 1980 to 2016. The effect per day is small against the funds' rate risk (the rate trend of 2022 to 2024 dominates it), and after 2016 no split is on file, so persistence is a claim to test.
3. **Outcome:** The fund's raw return (benchmark none) over the 3 sessions of the pre-month-end window from the entry close, against its own return over the same number of sessions outside every window; no cost is deducted from a timed position (ADR 0073).
4. **Trigger timing:** The window is fixed by the exchange calendar ahead of time (the last 3 sessions of the month), so nothing is read at D: the entry session S is the session before the first of them (start_offset_sessions = 1 from D = S - 1) and the return runs from S's close over 3 sessions. No fact after D places a window or a stretch of the base.
5. **Faithful replication:** The source's rule: hold long-duration Treasuries (TLT, IEF) for the last 2 to 3 sessions of the month and stay out otherwise (Etula et al 2020; Schwarz, Wharton Rodney White Center, 2019). Here three funds are held over the last 3 sessions, and the cash leg is only in the run record's timing Sharpe ratio.
6. **Expected size and sample:** Etula et al (2020): Treasuries show the mirror image of the equity sell-off around the turn of the month in 1980 to 2016; the page gives no basis-point figure it verified, and a Sharpe near 1 after costs for Schwarz's strategy comes from a summary of a paper it did not read. A split after 2016 is not on file. About 100 windows since 2018 give a t statistic that cannot separate a few basis points a day from zero when the funds' own rate moves are far larger.
7. **Capacity and costs:** Unlimited capacity in three liquid funds, about 12 round trips a year, 1 to 2 bps a side; long only. No cost is deducted in this version. The funds carry rate risk: a few days of rising yields wipe out many windows of a few basis points.
8. **Failure modes and retirement:** The effect is small against the funds' own volatility and the rate trend (2022 to 2024) dominates it; the Treasury auction calendar and Fed meetings land on the same dates; index extension is public, so front-running may have taken it. Retire if the frozen-period in-window minus out-of-window mean is not above zero for TLT and IEF, or the Welch t over the independent windows stays under 2.
9. **Decoys:** The equity turn-of-the-month effect (its mirror image), the Fed-meeting drift and the Treasury auction cycle (auctions cluster mid-month, so separable): test against the 'any 3-session window' null, which the 1,000 random draws of the harness are.

**Sources**

- Etula, E., Rinne, K., Suominen, M. and Vaittinen, L., Dash for Cash: Monthly Market Impact of Institutional Liquidity Needs, Journal of Finance, 2020 <https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/00fea0ae-963b-444c-8221-912121905b98>
- Schwarz, K., Wharton Rodney White Center working paper 17-19 (the end-of-month Treasury coupon strategy), 2019 <https://rodneywhitecenter.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/17-19.Schwarz.pdf>

## Premium closed-end funds that over-distribute (`cef_distribution_trap`)

**Status:** blocked. **Family:** H. **Thesis:** Closed-end funds at a large premium to NAV whose distributions exceed what they earn fall in price when the payout is cut, so they are ones to avoid.

**Why blocked:** Two things are missing. Closed-end funds are not in the covered universe (ADR 0013: security_types of config/site/universe.toml; a fund typed FUND is the reference's CEF and is not in the nightly bars), so there are no closes, outcomes or screener rows; the owner decision and the bars-history run are the same as for closed_end_fund_discounts. And the rule's second half, distribution coverage (the payout against earned income), needs the fund's distribution history and its Section 19(a) notices: no CEF dividend rows are stored (dividends follow the covered universe) and 19(a) notices are not ingested. The premium half is available through cef_nav@v1 (cef_discount_to_nav above 0.10) once bars exist; that alone is a weaker rule than the practitioner's, and a premium-only screener should be filed as that proxy, named.

- **Outcome:** excess return, over 60, 125, 250 sessions, vs SPY, entered 1 session after the decision session, costs 50 bps
- **Schedule:** month end
- **Universe:** `instrument.security_type eq 'CEF'` and `instrument.status eq 'ACTIVE'` and `rollup.price_stats@v2.close gt 3` and `rollup.price_stats@v2.adv_usd_20d gte 1000000`
- **Top K:** 10
- **Screeners:** none yet
- **Baselines:** `size_small`
- **Frozen period:** from 2026-04-01
- **Notes:** Short side, owner decision O2 (2026-10-17): a short result on liquid names may be a verdict basis. Small funds are expensive to short (borrow plus the distribution owed) and rarely have an ADV over the capacity floor; the useful form for an individual is an avoid filter on closed_end_fund_discounts, never to buy a premium fund with a payout above its coverage. Fund edges are keyed by instrument_id (ADR 0018), never by hand-listed tickers. Practitioner rule only (premium above 10% and coverage below 90%): no published return, so nothing is filed under [published].

**Quality bar**

1. **Mechanism:** Retail buys the headline yield; return of capital masks the erosion of the asset value, and the cut becomes unavoidable once the NAV has fallen far enough (PIMCO's PGP and PHK are the practitioner cases, with premiums of 40 to 70% and price falls of 20 to 40% on the day).
2. **Persistence:** Yield-chasing is behavioural and encouraged by sponsors; shorting a premium fund costs borrow plus the distribution owed, so arbitrageurs do not lean on it. Evidence is a case series, not a panel, and no independent 2018 to 2025 panel was found.
3. **Outcome:** Minus the excess return over SPY over 60, 125 and 250 sessions after the entry close, net of 50 bps a round trip and the assumed borrow: a short position in the premium funds with the weakest payout coverage.
4. **Trigger timing:** The NAV is known from the session after its date (events/fund_nav known_from) and the premium pairs it with the close of its own date; a distribution notice is public when issued. The rank is read at D (the last session of the month), the entry session is S = D + 1 (start_offset_sessions = 1).
5. **Faithful replication:** The practitioner rule (Morningstar and CEFA, not verified): premium above 10% and distribution coverage below 90% means a negative expected price return, exit on any announced cut. The premium quintile in Patro, Piccotti and Wu supplies the short leg of their long-short spread (a two-group figure, not filed). No screener is written until closes and payout data exist.
6. **Expected size and sample:** No published panel number. The case series show price falls of 20 to 40% on a cut day, at funds that held large premiums for years; the premium itself predicts negative price returns in the quintile sort. Few funds ever hold a 10% premium, so the independent count is the binding limit.
7. **Capacity and costs:** Shorting a small fund costs borrow plus the distribution owed and may not be possible; the 50 bps cost and the site's assumed borrow stand in for it. As an avoid filter capacity is irrelevant.
8. **Failure modes and retirement:** A premium that persists for years (a fund can stay at a premium while it distributes); a short squeeze in a small fund; a rights offering or merger that resets the premium. Retire if the frozen-period short return of the picks is not above the mean of every eligible fund.
9. **Decoys:** The mirror of the discount edge (a premium is a narrow discount: compare with closed_end_fund_discounts' ranking on the same sessions), leverage and rate beta, and size.
